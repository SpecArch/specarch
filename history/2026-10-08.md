## Derived cases ranked by harm and by mistakes

The first of the seven items in `docs/test-generation.md`. A requirement
may name its `harm` (data-loss, money, security, safety, privacy,
availability), and a field or a parameter's schema may say `mistakes:
frequent` or `mistakes: rare`. Every derived case has a frequency from the
table now printed in `docs/conventions.md`, and a rank: critical when its
subject satisfies a requirement with a harm or when it is a failing
dependency, frequent when its frequency is frequent, other otherwise.

`test_case_missing` now warns only for critical and frequent cases. On an
operation with no harm the boundary cases, the 4xx and 5xx responses and
the duplicates drop out of the warnings; a missing required field, a
caller without the permission, a pattern, an enum, a format, a not-found
lookup, a wrong state and a command's exit codes stay. The test plan has a
section "Derived cases left out" with every uncovered case of rank other,
its subject, its scenario and the reason. The traceability document and
chapter 13 of the techspec show a Harm column once a requirement names a
harm, and the requirements document names the harm of each requirement.
A field's `mistakes` shows among its limits in the techspec.

Specification first: SA-21 and SA-22 with NEED-9, ADR-020, ISO/IEC/IEEE
29119-4:2021 as a source, the rule text of `test_case_missing`, `harm` and
`mistakes` in the design schema. Both validator builds rank the same way
and print the same output; the documents are Go only. Six cases lost the
warnings they no longer get, `validate-derived-cases-listed` among them,
and five cases are new: harm making every case chosen, `mistakes` rare and
frequent with a failing channel, an unknown harm, the left-out section,
and the Harm column. In the example, LIB-3 names `harm: [money]`.

Decisions made here, for the owner to confirm or veto:

- A subject takes the harm of its own `satisfies` only. A constraint or a
  transition does not take the harm of its entity, so marking one does
  not mark the rest.
- Every derived case of a critical subject is critical, the golden
  boundary cases too, not only the red ones the design text first named.
- The Harm column appears only when some requirement names a harm, so a
  specification that does not use harm gets no empty column.
- The left-out table has a Scenario column where the design had Rank,
  since every row there has rank other.
- `mistakes` on a parameter's schema covers its `not found` case as well
  as its limits.

Open: the v0.2.0 schema, which the editor hints name, has neither `harm`
nor `mistakes`, so an editor flags both until the next release tag.

## What SpecArch takes from dxlib, and idioms

A design item, read-only in dxlib, dxlib_module and the two services built on
them. Two documents came out of it: `docs/dxlib-lessons.md`, the study the
owner asked for, and `docs/idioms.md`, the design of the idiom library the
owner decided on the same day. Nothing in dxlib was changed, and no code or
schema changed here; the README lists both documents and the roadmap
points at them from the SQL target and the 0.2 candidates.

The study starts, as the owner asked, from dxlib's unified type definition:
one struct rendered to the request validator, to JSON, to Go and to four SQL
dialects. The verdict is to adopt the model and not the encoding. The design
type stays SpecArch's structural one, since the keywords already say what
dxlib's fifty-five names say and cannot have the gaps a name registry has;
the rendering becomes a shipped, overridable table, the first idiom, keyed by
type, format, width and precision. The four-dialect SQL table is copied with
each engine divergence and the reason dxlib wrote beside it. Two findings
decided the home of the table: dxlib's own `JSONType` and `GoType` fields
are read by nothing, while the two renderings in use live where their
readers are; and the ERP backend has written two SpecArch-shaped YAML
languages of its own on top of dxlib, because the Go declarations were not
reviewable.

The map found the design side already holds entities, enums, operations,
permissions and state machines, and lacks what every dxlib service has:
field sensitivity and masking, encryption at rest, audit fields and soft
delete, one shape for list operations, background jobs, a standard error
body, limits, menus and a read model. Each is proposed as a 0.2 keyword with
its validator rule, and the error body takes RFC 9457 rather than dxlib's
shape, with the reason.

A Go implementation on dxlib is designed as a second Go route beside plain
Go: a `dialect: dxlib` setting on the OpenAPI target, since dxlib already
emits, reads and binds that document with drift fatal at start, and a
`specarch-gen-go-dxlib` for the model, table and handler declarations. Two
mismatches were read and are stated rather than wished away: dxlib's reader
refuses the JSON Schema bounds SpecArch writes, and dxlib routes by URI
alone. Both are dxlib items. The notification service is named as the real
project a go-dxlib generator would be accepted against; the umbrella
repository's focus rule leaves it waiting. Job C branches on whether the
private service is on dxlib, which could not be read from here.

Idioms are templates shipped in `idioms/`, embedded in the binary and
versioned with SpecArch; a project uses them by default, declares only
exclusions and overrides under `idioms` in its implementation file, and
keeps overrides as files beside it, each recording the shipped version it
was copied from. The lookup order is override, shipped rendering for the
stack, shipped stack-neutral part. The validator warns when a shipped
template is newer than an override's base. The first set of fifteen is
written from dxlib, each row saying what a tool checks and what is
guidance.

Decisions made here, for the owner to confirm or veto:

- An override may add contract statements and may not remove or weaken a
  shipped one; a different contract is an exclusion plus the project's own
  idiom under another name.
- A list on an engine without an array type renders as JSON text with the
  reason in the row, as dxlib does, rather than failing generation.
- A UUID column renders as the engine's native type where one exists and as
  36-character text elsewhere; a duration as an interval where one exists
  and as ISO 8601 text elsewhere.
- `format: time`, a time of day, joins the type list.
- An enum column is text plus a check constraint on all four engines, not
  the native enum types.

Left out on purpose: the names of a former private dependency and its owner
that a comment in dxlib_module mentions, the ticket and design references in
dxlib's comments, the product names in the operation ids of dxlib's OpenAPI
examples, and the country whose tax number dxlib validates. The handoff
mailbox was empty.

Open: whether the private service of job C runs on dxlib, which picks the Go
route; and whether the notification service's specification lives in its own
repository, which the focus rule holds for now.

## Records beside the specification

Step 2 of `docs/maintenance.md` is built in both validator builds. A
specification's records sit in `records/` beside its folder, the place
approvals already used: one file per change request, defect, release,
incident, commissioning run or approval, in a folder per kind. They have
their own schema, `schema/specarch-record-0.1.schema.json`, and eight new
rules: `record_name`, `record_ref`, `change_applied`, `change_decision`,
`defect_test`, `defect_duplicate`, `incident_link` (a warning) and
`commissioning_record`. Two new source kinds, `change-set` and
`defect-set`, let a record name an ID kept in an external tracker. A
`records/` folder inside the specification's own folder is a layout error
that says to move it up a level. SpecArch's own specification gains SA-23
for this. library-lending has a release, a commissioning run, an incident,
the defect it found and two change requests, one approved and one
rejected.

Four points the design left open are settled:

- A change's `affects` and a defect's `violates` hold a requirement ID or
  a `#/` pointer, nothing else. The design listed bare test, environment
  and setting names as well, but `production` could be an environment, a
  test and a setting at once, and the principles make a reference that can
  mean two things an error. A test is `#/tests/<name>`, and so on.
- A defect that turns out not to be a defect names the change request it
  became under `change`.
- An incident says whether the rollback was used in `rollbackUsed`, and
  names the role that reported it in `reportedBy` when no monitor caught
  it; the schema needs one of the two.
- Approvals, which `specarch approve` already writes, are a sixth kind of
  record and are checked like the rest, so the role that approved must be
  a stakeholder.

That last point turned up a fixture that could not happen: the case
`generate-with-plugin` held an approval by `owner` in a specification with
no stakeholders, which `approve` itself would refuse. The case now
declares the stakeholder, and its approval's digest was recomputed.

`record_ref` leaves three references to other rules so that none is
reported twice: a change's `affects` belong to `change_applied`, a
defect's `duplicateOf` to `defect_duplicate`, and a commissioning run's
check names to `commissioning_record`. A requirement in an external
requirement set counts as present and never as removed, since the
validator cannot read the set. A defect's test passes `defect_test` when
it verifies a requirement the defect violates, or when its subject is the
element a violated pointer points into.

The release rules (`release_contents`, `release_bump`, `release_version`)
are step 3 and are not checked yet; a release record is read for its
references only.

Twelve new cases, recorded from a run of the Go build: one valid tree with
every kind, the trackers, the schema, one per rule, and the layout error.
go vet, go test, swift test (13 pass), validate on `spec` and `examples`
in both builds and `document --check` for all seven targets pass. The two
builds print the same on all 160 case folders. No dependency change. The
handoff mailbox was empty.

## Release rules, and SpecArch's own releases

Step 3 of `docs/maintenance.md` is built in both validator builds: three
rules over the release records. `release_contents` holds a released
release to changes that are implemented or released and defects that are
fixed or released, and holds a released change or defect to a released
release that includes it. `release_bump` checks each released version
against the one before by the largest impact of what it includes, a
defect counting as patch. `release_version` checks that `info.version` is
the newest released version, or a later pre-release whose release is
planned, and that a release's `specificationVersion` is its version; that
second check was added here, since the design says the two are equal and
nothing else would catch it. SpecArch's specification gains SA-24 and
declares Semantic Versioning 2.0.0 as a source.

One decision, written into the design: before 1.0.0 a major change needs
the minor step, 0.3.0 after 0.2.x. SemVer's rule 4 asks for no step at all
in major version zero, so a literal rule would check nothing for a young
system; the minor step is what Cargo and npm's caret ranges already treat
as breaking under 1.0.0. It is the owner's to veto. Versions are ordered by
SemVer's precedence, a pre-release below its normal version.

On the owner's choice, SpecArch's own version line now follows the rule.
`records/releases/` at the repository root holds 0.1.0 (2026-10-07,
1c1c819) and 0.2.0 (2026-10-08, 1c3d233) as released and 0.3.0 as planned
for the work on main since the tag. `info.version` and both implementation
files' `implements.version` are 0.3.0-dev, and the documents carry it. The
program still prints 0.2.0 until the next tag. The 0.2.0 record gives no
`specificationVersion`, because the specification said 0.1.0 when that
tag was made and the record would otherwise state what was not true. No
change or defect records exist for either release; what they carried is
in git and in this history.

Four new cases: a valid line across 1.0.0, and one per rule. go vet, go
test, swift test (13 pass), validate on `spec` and `examples` in both

## The diff verb

Step 4 of `docs/maintenance.md` is built, in Go only: `specarch diff <old>
<new>` compares two specification folders element by element and checks
the release of the new version. The Swift build answers it with exit
status 2, as it does `document` and `generate`, and its implementation
file says so. SpecArch's specification gains the command and SA-25.

The design section "The diff verb" settles what the item left open. An
element is one named object of a section, one operation, one source or one
single section; `info`, `stages` and `specarch` are not compared. The
public interface is every operation, command, channel and setting, the
route of every page, and every entity and enum those reach through `$ref`;
everything else is patch whatever happens to it. A changed public element
takes the largest impact of its changes by a table of rules: descriptive
keys are patch, an enum value or an optional item added is minor, a value
removed or a name newly required is major, and any other difference is
major with its reason printed. That last rule is cautious on purpose: a
bound that moves may be safe for what a client sends and not for what it
receives, and the diff cannot tell which side a schema is on. The records
read are the new side's, and the release is the record of the new
`info.version` without its pre-release tag.

The version helpers moved from the validator into `internal/semver`, so
`release_bump` and `diff` measure a step the same way, with the step
before 1.0.0 as before; a unit test runs SemVer's own precedence example.

A trial run from the v0.2.0 tag to main, not kept, shows what the owner
should know about the coverage check: 36 elements changed since the tag,
the largest a minor (the new `diff` command, and `validate`'s `reads` list
counted as major by the cautious rule), so 0.3.0 is step enough; but none
of the 36 is named by a change request, since SpecArch keeps none, and the
check reports every one. Whether SpecArch's own releases will carry change
records, or the check should leave patch-level elements alone, is the
owner's call; the design keeps the check as it was written.

Eight new cases: the change list, the classification rules, a step too
small, an element no record names, a release of tracker IDs only (a
warning), no release record, an invalid specification and a usage error.
go vet, go test, swift test (13 pass), validate on `spec` and `examples` in
both builds and `document --check` for all seven targets pass; the two
builds print the same on all 243 validate runs over the case folders. No
dependency change. The handoff mailbox was empty.

## Structured test data

Step 3 of `docs/test-generation.md` is built in both validator builds. A
design test may carry `fixture` (the caller and the records that exist),
`input` (what the call carries) and `expect` (status, body, exit, standard
output, the state after, the messages published or `emitsNothing`), in the
design's own vocabulary, beside its `given`, `when` and `then`. The new
rule `test_data` checks them against the design: the caller is a role or
public; records name entities and fields that exist, with values of their
types, and hold under the entity's check constraints; an input names the
operation's parameters and body fields, the command's arguments and
options, or the page's route parameters and an action's label; an
expected status, exit code, body field and message are ones the subject
has. A test with `input` and an `input/` folder, or `expect` and an
`expected/` folder, is refused, so there is one way to say each thing.
SpecArch's specification gains SA-27.

Settled here, and written into the design: `status` and `exit` are whole
numbers; a record names only the fields that matter, and a constraint
naming a field it leaves out is not evaluated; an object or list value is
not type-checked, since a test names it only as a whole; a page's
`action` is the label of one of its actions, the only name an action
has.

library-lending's `lend-a-copy` test now carries a fixture, input and
expected outcome as the example. Three new cases. go vet, go test, swift
test (13 pass), validate on `spec` and `examples` in both builds and
`document --check` for all nine targets pass; the two builds print the
same on all 256 validate runs. No dependency change. The handoff mailbox

## specarch derive

## The waiting red paths

Step 6 of `docs/test-generation.md` is built in both validator builds. Five
red paths a reviewer asks for had no words in the meta-model, so no test
could be derived for them: a dependency down or slow, a request sent
twice, a record past its date, a session past its limit, two writers on
one record. Each now has the smallest concept a test can be derived from.

- `dependencies` is a design section of external systems, each with a
  `timeout`, and an operation lists what it `calls`. The cases are
  `dependency fails <name>` and `dependency times out <name>`, critical
  whatever the operation's harm, with the operation's 502, 503 or 504
  response as the outcome when it declares one.
- `idempotencyKey` on a POST or PATCH names a header parameter of the
  operation. The cases are the golden `repeated with the same <key>` and
  the red `<key> reused for another request`. On GET, PUT or DELETE the
  keyword is refused, since RFC 9110 already makes them idempotent.
- `validity` on an entity names the fields that bound when a record is
  current, `from` and `until`, both dates or both instants. An operation
  whose body names such a record through a relation gets `expired
  <field>` and, with `from`, `not yet valid <field>`.
- `session` declares an `idleTimeout`, an `absoluteTimeout` or both, the
  two the OWASP cheat sheet asks for. Every operation, command and page
  whose permission is not public then gets `denied with expired session`.
- `guard` on an operation or a command names the entity the change writes,
  a `precondition` over its fields, and the exact `recordsChanged`. The
  cases are `guard precondition fails` and `concurrent write`, the latter
  critical on its own. The precondition is checked as a check constraint
  is.

Every duration is an ISO 8601 duration in days, hours, minutes and seconds,
never zero, written as a pattern in the schema rather than JSON Schema's
`format: duration`: the Go schema library asserts that format and the
Swift one does not, so the narrow form is what both builds check alike.
Weeks, months and years are left out because their length depends on the
calendar.

Specification first: SA-29 with NEED-9, ADR-021, the sources `rfc-9110`,
`ietf-idempotency-key` and `owasp-session-management`, five new rules
(`dependency`, `idempotency_key`, `validity`, `session`, `guard`) in the
rule enum and in both builds. The techspec gains a Dependencies table in
the context chapter, a Sessions section among the cross-cutting concepts,
the validity on each entity, and each operation's calls, idempotency and
guard, with the dependencies drawn in the sequence diagram. The
conventions carry a section per concept and the new rows of the derived
case table; `docs/stages.md`, `docs/roadmap.md`, `docs/generators.md` and
`docs/test-generation.md` say the concepts exist. Six new conformance
cases, five red and one golden, and two test plan cases re-recorded for
the one sentence of the left-out section that changed. The library
lending example uses all five, with a fee ledger, an idempotency key on
lending, a membership end date, a session and a guard on return and loss,
and 23 tests written for the chosen cases, most of them drafted by
`specarch derive` and then completed by hand.

Decisions made here, for the owner to confirm or veto:

- The meta-model stays at 0.1, as it did for harm, mistakes and the
  structured test data; the keywords are additions no existing file breaks
  on. The v0.2.0 schema the editor hints name has none of them, so an
  editor flags them until the next release tag.
- The time limit sits on the dependency, not on the call site, so one
  system has one limit wherever it is called.
- Validity sits on the entity and not on a field, since a record is
  current or not as a whole; the item named both placements.
- The failing and slow dependency and the concurrent write are critical on
  their own; the expired record, the reused key and the failed
  precondition are occasional, so they are chosen only on a subject with
  a harm.
- A guard's postcondition is not a derived case, because a test cannot set
  it up from outside; `recordsChanged` is for the emitted script.
- A command may carry a guard, since the guarded operational scripts are
  commands; a command does not call dependencies yet.

go vet, go test, swift test (13 pass), validate on `spec` and `examples`
in both builds and `document --check` for all nine targets pass; the two
builds print the same on all 126 validate cases and on both
specifications. No dependency change. The handoff mailbox was empty. The
untracked `internal/gentests/gentests.go` belongs to the paused Go test
plug-in item and is left out of this commit.

An agent in another repository can now write the specification of an
existing system from its prose documents and its Go code, end to end,
with the toolchain checking each step. `docs/from-sources.md` is the
procedure, with how to install and pin `specarch` and a short block of
agent instructions to paste into that repository.

What the procedure needed and did not have:

- A source of kind `code`: a system's code read at one commit, named as
  its edition, cited by file and line or by package and function. A
  `system` source is a running system that was observed, which code is
  not.
- `clauses` on a source: the outline of a document, or the files of the
  code. `specarch gaps`, and the `questions` document, gain a Coverage
  section listing per source the elements each clause produced, how
  many clauses produced nothing, and the citations outside the outline.
- `why`, `cites`, `origin` and `decidedIn` on a mapping of an
  implementation file, so the as-built mappings cite the code lines they
  were read from. Both builds check them through the existing origin
  rules, with no code change.

The four cases of two sources use what already existed: where they agree,
one element cites both; where they disagree, a must question cites both
and the disputed key waits; what only the code has is inferred, with a why
that starts "Undocumented, from code", and a should question; what only
the documents have is stated, with a should question in the
implementation stage that starts "Not built yet".

Specification first: SA-30, ADR-022, and the `gaps` command's design.
Two new conformance cases: `validate-mapping-origin` in both builds, and
`gaps-coverage`. The new example `examples/lending-desk` is the procedure
run on a public sample: a short desk manual and a small Go service with
its own `go.mod`, so the root module does not build it. They disagree on
the loan period (21 days against 14), the service serves a route the
manual never mentions, and the manual describes renewals the service does
not have. The specification has five open questions, and its documents
are generated. The sample's validate run prints 22 warnings for the tests
it has not written; the fifth question says so, and they stay.

Decided here, for the owner to confirm or veto:

- A citation outside its source's clauses is a line in `gaps`, not yet a
  validator rule; the rule waits for a real project.
- Undocumented and not built are not new origins but inferred and stated
  elements with a should question, so there is one way to say each.

Pinning: no tag is needed to start. A private repository installs
`go install github.com/SpecArch/specarch/cmd/specarch@<commit>` at the
commit that carries this work, and checks the module version and its h1
checksum with `go version -m`. A v0.3.0 tag would give it a plain name.

## Tests 5/7: the Go test plug-in

`specarch generate tests` now writes the Go tests of a specification
through `specarch-gen-tests-go`, the first plug-in in this repository
(step 5 of `docs/test-generation.md`). It writes one file,
`specarch_design_test.go`, with one test per design test and one per
worked example. The file declares a `Harness` interface that the project
implements beside it, through `newHarness(t)`: signing a caller in,
inserting and reading records through an entity's mapping, calling an
operation, running a command, opening a page, and computing an
algorithm. A design test with a fixture, an input and an expected
outcome becomes a complete test; one without a call calls a
`body<TestName>` function the project writes, so the package does not
compile until every such body exists. Values carry their type, and
decimals are compared as decimals.

`generate` now looks up a plug-in per implementation file:
`specarch-gen-<target>-<stack>` first, the stack being the file's
language in lower case, then `specarch-gen-<target>`. Files that find the
same plug-in run it together, and `--out` is refused when they reach more
than one. The design had named the plug-in through
`targets.tests.tool`, but `tool` is the downstream tool that takes a
generator's output, so the stack in the name carries it instead, and the
Swift and Flutter plug-ins of step 7 follow the same rule.

Specification first: SA-31, the generate design, and the Go
implementation file's layout and a `generated-tests` suite. Two
conformance cases, `generate-stack-plugin` and `generate-stack-fallback`.
No real Go project has a hand-written test to accept the generator
against yet, so the unit test generates the library lending example's

and an empty body for each test without a call. That test found the
prelude being passed through a format string, which is fixed.

## dxlib 1/8: idioms, and the type rendering of every field

SpecArch now ships idioms, and the first one: `type-rendering`, what each
field type of the design becomes in Go and in PostgreSQL, SQL Server,
Oracle and MariaDB. This is item 1 of the dxlib study and items 1 to 3 of
`docs/idioms.md`.

- The idiom format has its own schema, `schema/specarch-idiom-0.1.schema.json`.
  The shipped idioms live under `idioms/` and are embedded in both builds.
  An implementation file gets them without a word; it excludes or
  overrides one under `idioms`, with the reason, and a project's override
  or its own idiom is a file in the `idioms/` folder beside it.
- Six rules, in Go and Swift with the same output: `idiom_unknown`,
  `idiom_part_unknown`, `idiom_override_reason`, `idiom_stack`,
  `idiom_version_behind` (a warning) and `idiom_contract`. The last refuses
  an override that changes a shipped contract statement, and any entity
  field that no row of the type rendering matches for a stack of the
  implementation file, such as a decimal wider than Oracle's 38 digits.
- `specarch idioms` lists the idioms each implementation file uses, and
  `specarch idioms diff <idiom>` prints the shipped rendering beside each
  override's. The techspec's implementation chapter gains an Idioms table.
- The `sql` target's `dialect` takes the four dialects.

The rows carry the owner's three points:

- Money is a decimal of the precision and scale the field needs, 23 and 4
  for a currency with many zeros. In Go it is `decimal.Decimal` from
  `github.com/shopspring/decimal` v1.4.0 (MIT), the library dxlib uses,
  never a float or a plain integer. In JSON it is a string, and in
  JavaScript it is never turned into a Number. SQL rows are NUMERIC or
  DECIMAL of the field's precision. Oracle and SQL Server stop at 38 digits
  and MariaDB at 65, so a wider field has no row and is refused.
- Oracle text is `VARCHAR2(N CHAR)`, never `VARCHAR`, up to 1000
  characters, and `CLOB` beyond that. Oracle's documentation gives the
  numbers: 4000 bytes under `MAX_STRING_SIZE = STANDARD` (the default) and
  32767 under `EXTENDED`. A length in characters is a limit rather than a
  capacity, so the column is only sure to hold N characters up to 1000, or
  8191 under EXTENDED, which a project sets in an override. A CLOB cannot
  be an index key, cannot be sorted, grouped, deduplicated or joined on,
  and cannot be compared with `=` in SQL. The row says so, citing the SQL
  Language Reference (Data Types), the Database Reference
  (MAX_STRING_SIZE) and the LOB restrictions.
- Absent and null are kept apart. `required: false` on a request field
  says it may be left out, and a type list with `null` says the value may
  be null. In Go, absence is a presence flag or an accessor and null is a
  pointer or a `sql.Null` type. dxlib's `nullable-X` on a parameter means
  absence, so its plain `int32` is deliberate.
  `docs/dxlib-lessons.md` called it a slip and now says otherwise. In SQL
  an entity field that is nullable or not required is a nullable column.

`format: time` was already in the schema; the conventions' Types table
now lists it.

Decided here, for the owner to confirm or veto (ADR-023):

- An implementation file's stacks are its language and the dialect of
  each target; a `sql` target without one is PostgreSQL.
- A row matches on the field's own keys. A row without a format matches
  only a format that no row of the rendering names, so a decimal never
  falls to the plain text row.
- A word in braces in a row's `render` is filled from the field by a
  generator. It is not a placeholder.
- An idiom file declares its own `sources`.
- `staysBehind: true` on an override silences `idiom_version_behind`,
  since free text in `why` cannot be checked.
- An override may add contract statements; one it repeats must be
  identical.
- Override problems are reported in the override's file. A missing row is
  reported at the implementation file's `stack` key, or at the target's
  `dialect`.
- SQL Server text is `NVARCHAR`, not dxlib's `VARCHAR`, because `VARCHAR`
  holds the collation's code page unless the collation is UTF-8.
- Oracle's time of day is `INTERVAL DAY(0) TO SECOND(0)`, not dxlib's
  `DATE`.

Left for later: `paginated-list` (it reads `listOf`, a 0.2 keyword of the
next item), the cases under an idiom's `tests` joining the derived cases,
each idiom's contract in the techspec's chapter 8, and `specarch-gen-sql`
rendering through the rows. The `exact-decimal` statement is checked by the
generators' `--check`, not by the validator, which checks that a row
exists and not what it renders. Spec first: SA-32,
ADR-023, the rule enum, the `idioms` and `idioms diff` commands. There are
seven new conformance cases, three `document` cases re-recorded for the
Idioms table, and a unit test of the shipped idioms. The generator file
`internal/generate/implementation.go` was already unformatted at HEAD and
is left as it was apart from the new table. No dependency of SpecArch
changed, since the decimal library is named for projects, not imported.
The handoff mailbox was empty.

go vet, go test, swift test (14 pass), validate on `spec` and `examples`
in both builds with the same output (and on all 129 validate cases), and
`document --check` pass.

## dxlib 2/8, part 1: sensitive data, encryption at rest, audit fields, soft delete

The first of three parts of item 2 of the dxlib study: the keywords about
stored data.

- `sensitivity` on a field: `public`, `internal`, `personal` or
  `credential`. A credential that a response can carry is refused unless
  the field is `writeOnly`, JSON Schema's word for a value that is sent
  and never returned. A personal field in a public operation's response is
  warned about (`sensitivity_exposed`). Request bodies are not checked,
  since a credential arrives in one by design.
- `atRest: encrypted` on a field, and `lookup: hash` with it. An encrypted
  key or unique field needs the lookup, and a lookup without encryption is
  refused (`at_rest`).
- `audited: true` and `deletion: soft` on an entity. The entity does not
  declare the fields the system sets (`createdAt`, `createdBy`,
  `lastModifiedAt`, `lastModifiedBy`, `deleted`), because the keyword
  already says them (`audited`). A list of a softly deleted entity derives
  `deleted <Entity> not listed`, and a read of one by id derives `deleted
  <Entity> read`, answered as not found.

The techspec says under each such entity that it is audited or softly
deleted. Chapter 8 lists every field that is not public or is encrypted.
The library lending example's member is audited and softly deleted, and
its name and email are personal, with the email encrypted and found by a
hash. Its two new derived cases are listed as left out, since no member
requirement names a harm.

Decided here, for the owner to confirm or veto (ADR-024): the audit fields
and the deleted flag are implied by the keyword and not declared, so a
test cannot yet name them in a fixture. Only responses are checked for
credentials. An encrypted field is a key or unique only through its hash.

Spec first: SA-33, ADR-024, the rule enum with three rules in both builds.
Two conformance cases. The masking, the engine's encryption and the column
names are the pii-in-logs, encrypted-column, audit-fields and soft-delete
idioms, not built yet. None of this overlaps item 024's keywords.

go vet, go test, swift test (14 pass), validate on `spec` and `examples`
in both builds with the same output (and on all 131 validate cases), and
`document --check` pass. No dependency change. The handoff mailbox was
empty.

## dxlib 2/8, part 2: lists, limits and problem types

The second part of item 2 of the dxlib study: the keywords about an
operation's interface.

- `listOf` on an operation names the entity, the fields a search matches
  (`searchable`), the fields a filter may name (`filterable`), the fields
  to sort by (`sortable`), and a `pageSize` with a `maximum` and a
  `default`. The fields must be the entity's. An encrypted field is never
  searched or sorted by, and is filtered by only with `lookup: hash`. The
  default page fits the maximum (`list_of`). The cases are `page beyond
  last`, `page size above <maximum>`, `sort by a field not sortable` and
  `filter by a field not filterable`.
- `limits` on an operation: `maxRequestBytes`, and a `rate` of
  `requests` `per` a duration with a `burst` of at least as many
  (`limits`). The cases are `request larger than <n> bytes` and `rate
  exceeded`.
- `errors`, a design section: a catalogue of RFC 9457 problem types, each
  with its status, title and condition. A response names one under
  `problem`, of its own status. Once the catalogue exists, every 4xx and
  5xx response names one (`problem`).

The techspec lists the problem types in the runtime view. Each operation
says what it lists, its limits and the types it refuses with. The library
lending example has a catalogue of five problem types named by its seven
error responses, a sortable, paged list of loans, and limits on lending.
`specarch derive` drafted the two lending cases the limits imply, and they
were completed by hand.

The Swift build's list of design keys lacked `calls`, `idempotencyKey`,
`guard` and `validity`, which the Go build has had since item 024. Both
now carry those and every keyword of this item, so an implementation file
that names one is reported the same way by both.

Decided here, for the owner to confirm or veto (ADR-025): a request
outside a list's whitelists is refused, not ignored. The catalogue is
optional, and a specification without one is not refused. A test of a
list carries no input for the paging parameters until the
`paginated-list` idiom names them. A rate is a duration in the form of
the timeouts, never zero.

Spec first: SA-34, ADR-025, the source `rfc-9457`, three rules in both
builds. Two conformance cases. The OpenAPI expansion of `listOf`, the
`rate-limit` idiom and the problem documents at run time wait for their
generators. None of this overlaps item 024's keywords.

go vet, go test, swift test (14 pass), validate on `spec` and `examples`
in both builds with the same output (and on all 133 validate cases), and
`document --check` pass. No dependency change. The handoff mailbox was
empty.

## dxlib 2/8, part 3: jobs and menus

The last part of item 2 of the dxlib study.

- `jobs`, a design section: work the system does on its own. A job has a
  description, a `trigger` that is exactly one of `schedule` (five cron
  fields, in UTC), `every` (a duration) or `consumes` (a channel and
  message), the `role` it acts as, the entities it `reads` and `writes`,
  the dependencies it `calls`, the messages it `emits`, and `retries` with
  a `limit` and what follows the last try. The role, the entities and the
  consumed message must exist (`job`). Emitted messages and called
  dependencies are checked as an operation's are.
- A test may name a job as its subject. A job gets the cases `runs
  twice` (critical on its own), the dependency cases for what it calls,
  and `an item fails every try` when it retries.
- `menus`, a design section: a tree of entries, each a title with a page
  or with entries of its own. Every page an entry opens must exist
  (`menu`).

The techspec describes each job in the runtime view, and lists the menu
under the pages with the permission each entry needs. The library lending
example gains its nightly `markOverdue` job, which acts as a new
`scheduler` role and publishes the LoanOverdue the loan's state machine
already waited for. It also gains a menu over its four list and form
pages. `specarch derive` drafted the job's two tests, and they were
completed by hand.

Overlap with item 024, as asked: a job reuses `calls` and `dependencies`
for its dependency cases, and `runs twice` is the job's side of what
`idempotencyKey` is for an operation; no keyword of 024 is defined
again. A `deletion: soft` write and a `guard` stay apart, since one is a
kind of delete and the other a precondition on any change.

Decided here, for the owner to confirm or veto (ADR-026): a trigger is
one of three. A cron schedule is checked for its five fields only, since
checking its values would need a library in both builds. Whether a job's
writes stay inside its role's permissions is not checked yet.

Spec first: SA-35, ADR-026, two rules in both builds. Two new conformance
cases and `validate-test-subject` re-recorded for the job in its message.
The read model, `views`, is item 034.

in both builds with the same output (and on all 135 validate cases), and
`document --check` pass. No dependency change. The handoff mailbox was
empty.

## dxlib 3/8: the OpenAPI document, standard dialect

`specarch generate openapi` now writes the OpenAPI 3.1 document of a
specification, through the second plug-in in this repository,
`specarch-gen-openapi`. A standard OpenAPI generator in strict mode writes
the server interface from it.

- The document is the design field for field. An entity or an enum is a
  schema under `components`, and an audited entity carries its four audit
  fields as read-only. SpecArch's own keywords become `x-specarch-`
  extensions; the rationale and the test hints are left out.
- A response that names a problem type answers `application/problem+json`
  with RFC 9457's problem schema and names its type. The catalogue is
  `x-specarch-problems`. A type without a URI of its own is named, not
  given an invented one.
- A list takes the names of the `paginated-list` idiom: `search`, `filter`
  (a deepObject of the filterable fields), `sort` (each sortable field,
  ascending or after a minus), `page` and `pageSize`, as query parameters
  on a GET and as body properties on another method. Its answer is wrapped
  in `{items, totalItems, totalPages}`, with the totals bounded to
  2^53 - 1.
- The security scheme is the one the target's settings name; without it
  there is none. Each operation keeps its permission as an extension.

`paginated-list` ships at 1.0.0 with those names and that envelope, under
the stack `any`. An idiom's `reads` now matches a keyword used anywhere in
the design, not only a section, in both builds, so the idiom applies
wherever `listOf` is used.

The plug-in protocol changed: each implementation file in a request now
carries `idioms`, the idioms it uses with the content of the idiom that
applies and the project's override, so a plug-in renders through an
override without reading the disk. A plug-in author needs to know this.

The library lending example's `openapi` target now writes into
`examples/library-lending/openapi/`, with a bearer scheme, and the
document is committed and kept current by `generate --check`. A
conformance case may now name the repository's plug-ins under `plugins`;
the Go harness builds them and puts them on PATH, and `generate-openapi`
is the first such case.

Decided here, for the owner to confirm or veto (ADR-027): the standard
dialect is the absence of a dialect on the target, and the dxlib dialect
of item 032 must keep a non-SQL dialect out of an implementation file's
stacks. A list by a method other than GET takes its paging in its body,
and a body that refers to an entity is refused. No security scheme is
assumed.

Left for later: the dxlib dialect (item 032), checking the document with
an OpenAPI validator (a new dependency and its scan), and server code,
which is the standard generator's. Spec first: SA-36, ADR-027, the
`generate` request's `idioms`, the Go layout and a `generated-openapi`
suite. One conformance case and two unit tests.

During the work a script truncated `cmd/specarch/conformance_test.go`; it
was restored from git before the edit was made again, and nothing else was
touched.

in both builds with the same output (and on all 135 validate cases),
`document --check` and `generate openapi --check` pass. No dependency
